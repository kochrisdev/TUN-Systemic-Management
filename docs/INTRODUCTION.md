# An introduction to TUN Systemic Management

[Documentation index](README.md) · [Getting started](GETTING-STARTED.md)

Imagine a support team introducing an AI assistant. It drafts replies quickly. The implementation works, staff approve its replies, and the sending service confirms delivery to its configured boundary. Yet customers return with the same problems and staff spend more time correcting drafts.

The organization needs to answer a different question from whether the software ran: is this initiative improving support, at an acceptable cost, within the authority people intended to delegate?

TUN Systemic Management proposes a shared way to answer that question.

## From intent to organizational responsibility

The team begins with an outcome: help customers resolve eligible issues with less effort. It names a human owner, records a baseline, and identifies what remains uncertain. It considers alternatives, including improving support guidance without adding AI.

A decision authorizes a limited experiment. The mandate describes the purpose and responsibility. A separate delegation defines what the assistant may access or do, who can approve external effects, the resource ceiling, when authority expires, and when to escalate.

The team observes both operational facts and product outcomes. A verified send establishes a scoped action effect. A customer outcome needs its own evidence. Neither a completed task list nor a fluent summary establishes the latter.

At review, the team may continue, change, expand, pause, or retire the initiative. The decision records its evidence, uncertainties, owner, and next review condition. Changes in authority follow a new review and the application's enforcement path.

## What makes the approach systemic

Management follows relationships across outcomes, investments, people, agents, policies, tools, actions, and evidence. A local efficiency gain may increase workload elsewhere. Several individually small delegations may exhaust a shared budget. A changed policy may affect many running initiatives.

The same loop can apply to a portfolio, a product, or one mandate. Each level has its own owner and evidence needs. Local discretion remains bounded by the authority actually delegated to that level.

## Relationship to the companion projects

TUN Systemic Design concerns how people understand and control AI behavior. TUN Systemic Engineering concerns how applications enforce authority and establish what happened. Management concerns why an organization pursues an outcome, who is accountable, how resources are assigned, and when the evidence calls for a different decision.

The three responsibilities overlap at deliberate interfaces. A management approval does not itself authorize a tool invocation. An engineering receipt does not itself prove organizational value. A presentation component does not make either claim true.

This repository proposes the management model and supplies templates plus an [experimental local pilot](PILOT.md). The pilot exercises a narrow record and review workflow with fixture identities and synthetic evidence. The narrative examples remain fictional; organizational adoption and real provider integration remain future work.
